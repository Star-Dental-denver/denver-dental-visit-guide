Tooth pain and swelling: what to notice before you call

Tooth pain is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Its timing, triggers and accompanying signs help the dental team understand how quickly you may need to be evaluated.

Describe painSwellingAt the visitSources

Describe the pattern—not just the number

A zero-to-ten pain score is useful, but it does not tell the whole story. Share whether the pain is sharp, dull, throbbing, pressure-like or electric. Explain whether it appears only when biting, lingers after cold or heat, wakes you at night, or is becoming more frequent. Note whether you can identify one tooth or only a general area.

Useful details
  • When symptoms began
  • Hot, cold, sweet or biting triggers
  • Whether pain stops when the trigger stops
  • Recent filling, crown, extraction or injury
Other changes
  • Bad taste or drainage
  • Gum bump or pimple
  • Fever or feeling unwell
  • Trouble opening the mouth

Where is the swelling?

Swelling limited to the gum near a tooth and swelling that changes the face or extends beneath the jaw are different situations. Tell the office whether swelling is inside the mouth, in the cheek, near the eye, under the tongue or below the jaw. Rapid progression, difficulty breathing or swallowing, neck swelling, confusion or severe illness warrants emergency medical attention.

Do not wait for a routine appointment if facial or neck swelling is spreading rapidly, breathing or swallowing is difficult, or you feel severely ill. Seek urgent medical help.

What can you do while arranging care?

Gently rinse with warm water and floss if food may be trapped. Avoid putting aspirin directly on the gum because it can irritate tissue. Use medication only as directed on its label and only if it is appropriate for your health history. These steps may improve comfort, but they do not identify or treat the cause.

Why examination and X-rays matter

Decay, a cracked tooth, an inflamed dental nerve, gum disease and an abscess can overlap in how they feel. A dentist may examine the teeth and gums, test biting or temperature response, and use appropriate X-rays to identify the tooth and surrounding bone. The recommendation could range from a restoration or root canal treatment to extraction, periodontal care or referral, depending on the findings.
